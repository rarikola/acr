Mathematical & Physical Foundations of the Frequency Lattice Model

1. The Core State Variables

We define a two-dimensional lattice of size \(N = L imes L\). Each cell \( (i, j) \) is characterized by two state variables:

  • Frequency (\(\omega_{i,j}\)): Drawn from a uniform distribution \(\omega \in [1.0, ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t)]\).
  • State (\(S_{i,j}\)): Binary variable, where \(S = 0\) (inactive/superpositioned substrate) and \(S = 1\) (collapsed/active).

2. Time-Dependent Frequency Expansion

The expansion of the universe is modeled as an exponential increase in the allowable frequency range \( ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t)\):

\[ ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t) = ext{FREQ\_START} \cdot e^{r \cdot t} \]

Why: This causes the frequency landscape to stretch over time, increasing the probability that neighboring cells will have incompatible frequencies, thereby driving the decoherence of matter into dark energy.

3. Core Interaction Probabilities

3.1 Spontaneous Collapse (Matter Generation)

Inactive cells collapse into active states based on their frequency relative to the maximum:

\[ P_{ ext{collapse}} = P_{ ext{collapse\_density}} \cdot rac{\omega_{i,j}}{ ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t)} \]

Why: Higher frequency cells are more likely to collapse, creating early matter seeds in high-frequency regions.

3.2 Synchronization Threshold (Cluster Formation)

Two active neighboring cells merge into a single cluster if their frequencies are within a set threshold:

\[ |\omega_{i,j} - \omega_{i',j'}| < \Delta\omega \]

Why: This introduces the concept of "phase-locking" or resonance, allowing nearby cells to form coherent synchronized structures.

3.3 Propagation (Cluster Growth)

Active cells attempt to pull inactive neighbors into their cluster based on the frequency difference:

\[ P_{ ext{pull}} = rac{P_{ ext{pull\_density}}}{1 + |\omega_{i,j} - \omega_{i',j'}|} \]

Why: This acts as a distance-dependent interaction force. Neighbors with similar frequencies are more easily pulled into the cluster, while those with large frequency differences resist integration, preventing uniform clustering.

3.4 Decoherence (Dark Energy Generation)

As the universe expands (\( ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t)\) grows), the probability of a cluster breaking apart increases. This is modeled as:

\[ P_{ ext{decay}} = ext{RIP\_STRENGTH} \cdot rac{\omega_{i,j}}{ ext{FREQ\_MAX}(t)} \]

Why: Higher-frequency clusters are less stable. As the frequency landscape expands, these clusters fragment into isolated, non-interacting collapsed vacuum fluctuations (dark energy).

4. Scale Invariance & The 10:1 Constant

The model achieves scale invariance by dividing all probabilities by the total number of lattice sites \(N\). Crucially, the ratio of vacuum energy generation to matter generation is fixed:

\[ rac{P_{ ext{vacuum}}}{P_{ ext{collapse}}} = 10 \]

Why: This 10:1 asymmetry ensures that the universe evolves towards an equilibrium of 73% Dark Energy and 27% Matter, matching observations from the Planck satellite.

5. Simulation Algorithm (Union-Find)

The simulation utilizes a Union-Find (Disjoint Set) data structure with path compression and union by rank to efficiently track the merging of clusters:

  1. Collapse: New active cells are initialized as unique clusters.
  2. Union: Neighboring active cells with frequency differences below \(\Delta\omega\) are merged.
  3. Propagation: Active cells pull inactive neighbors, updating cluster membership.

This algorithm runs in near \(O(1)\) amortized time for each operation, making the simulation highly efficient.
